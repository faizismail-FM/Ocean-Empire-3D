# Research notes and verification status

The historical database in `src/data/historical` was compiled from the implementer's knowledge of the published literature, without live library or web access during implementation. In line with the design brief (Part 7.3), nothing here is presented as verified merely because it exists in the database. This file records what is well supported, what is interpretive, what has since been checked (October 2026, against library catalogues, publisher records, published reviews and reference works, with the sources named on each record), and what remains open because the scholarship itself is unsettled.

## Evidence ratings used

| Rating | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `established` | Widely accepted on documentary or archaeological evidence |
| `scholarly_interpretation` | Supported by evidence but dependent on historians' interpretation |
| `reconstruction` | A reasonable reconstruction made to make the game playable |
| `uncertain` | Disputed, incomplete or unverified |
| `fictional` | Gameplay invention, not a historical claim |

## Claims that need further research

### The Alexandrian Fleet (Rome), 112 CE
- **Bibliography**: Casson, *Ships and Seamanship in the Ancient World* (Princeton, 1971), Sidebotham, *Berenike and the Ancient Maritime Spice Route* (California, 2011), De Romanis, *The Indo-Roman Pepper Trade and the Muziris Papyrus* (Oxford, 2020, xxvi + 381 pp.), Keay, Millett, Paroli and Strutt, *Portus* (British School at Rome, 2005), Peacock and Blue (eds.), *Myos Hormos – Quseir al-Qadim* vol. 1 (Oxbow, 2006) and Pucci Ben Zeev, *Diaspora Judaism in Turmoil, 116/117 CE* (Peeters, 2005) were confirmed against catalogue, publisher and journal records in November 2026. Casson's *Periplus Maris Erythraei* (1989), Rickman, *The Corn Supply of Ancient Rome* (1980), Tomber, *Indo-Roman Trade* (2008), Young, *Rome's Eastern Trade* (2001), Meiggs, *Roman Ostia* (1973), Bowersock, *Roman Arabia* (1983), Munro-Hay, *Aksum* (1991) and Holum et al., *King Herod's Dream* (1988) are recorded from the implementer's knowledge and say so on the entry.
- **Dates checked**: Trajan's canal (a tax for its excavation attested in papyri of 112–114; "no later than 112"), the hexagonal basin at Portus (built about 100–112, commemorated on coins of 112–114), the Diaspora revolt (116–117 in Pucci Ben Zeev's chronology, which the game follows; 115–117 traditionally). Hadrian's accession (11 August 117), Antinoopolis (30 October 130), the Bar Kokhba war (132–135), Antoninus's accession (10 July 138) and death (7 March 161), the Farasan inscription (144) and the Muziris papyrus (mid second century) follow the standard accounts and were not re-checked; the Koptos tariff of 90 CE is mentioned in the Coptos entries but is outside the window.
- **The canal as a sea lane** is the campaign's central abstraction: seagoing ships pass from Alexandria by the Nile and Trajan's canal to the Red Sea, where in reality the India cargoes went overland from Coptos to Myos Hormos and Berenike by camel. The scenario note, the canal event and the Coptos roads technology all say so. The lane nodes are dated 112 to 767, when the canal is last recorded in use, so no later campaign can use them.
- **Muziris** is placed at Pattanam (probable, not certain) and the port is rated `disputed`; the Chera, Himyarite and Aksumite extents are coarse and rated `uncertain` or `scholarly_interpretation`; Nahapana's dates are disputed and he is rated `uncertain`.
- **Ships**: the new `roman_merchant` hull pattern (broad hull, swan-neck stern post, steering oars, square mainsail with a triangular topsail and an artemon) draws the corbita, the grain ship and the Red Sea merchantman; the actuaria uses the galley builder. Sizes follow Casson's ranges and Lucian's Isis; the Red Sea merchantman is a reconstruction, no Red Sea wreck of the period having been excavated.
- **Wind**: a single Mediterranean table is used for the whole map, so the monsoon appears only as the Hippalus technology's speed bonus; the scenario note says so.
- **Enslaved people** were bought and sold in every port of this world; the game does not represent them as cargo and the scenario note says so.
- **Rivals** (the merchants of Puteoli, the shipowners of Muza, the shippers of Barygaza) are reconstructions of documented communities, not particular firms.

### Siraf, 850 CE
- **Kalah's location** is unresolved. Checked October 2026: the Princeton Geniza Lab summarises a debate running since 1718, with proposals along the peninsula's west coast from Phuket and Takuapa to Kedah and Klang; Goitein's Kedah identification is described there as somewhat arbitrary. The port stays at an approximate Kedah-coast position and flagged `disputed`; the note on the record now says so.
- **Aden in the ninth century**: checked October 2026. Margariti dates Aden's emergence as a major emporium to the ninth and tenth centuries, as the India trade shifted toward the Red Sea; Ibn Khurradādhbih's account of the Rādhānite route (846) runs through al-Qulzum and Jedda without naming Aden. The record stays `uncertain`, with the reasoning on the record.
- **Basra's medieval site**: the early city lay near modern al-Zubayr; coordinates are approximate.
- **Daybul = Banbhore**: checked October 2026. The identification rests on F. A. Khan's 1958–1965 excavations and philological arguments (Baluch, Mughal); earlier proposals were Karachi (Elliot) and Kakar Bukera (Haig, on a mistranslation of al-Balādhurī). A Pakistani–Italian–French team reopened Banbhore in 2010 after doubts about Khan's dating, and the 2019 *Sindh Antiquities* special issue still calls the question open. Coordinates stay approximate.
- **Manda**: the settlement's name in contemporary Arabic sources is unknown; "Manda" is the modern island name.
- **Star-altitude navigation** in the ninth century is inferred from later practice (the kamal and Ibn Mājid's manuals are 15th century); rated `uncertain`.
- **Ship classes**: only the Belitung wreck is excavated. The "large sewn-plank ship" and "coastal vessel" are reconstructions; the word "dhow" is avoided as anachronistic for the period (Agius 2008).
- **Ambergris and pearls** are mentioned by Abu Zayd; the quantities, prices and cargo-space values in the game are abstractions.
- **Siraf earthquake c. 977**: checked October 2026. Wilkinson (1974) dates the earthquake to 977 and treats it as the turning point after which trade moved to Qais; al-Muqaddasī, writing at the end of the century, still praised the houses while noting the exodus. A 2020 geoarchaeological study of the harbour instead attributes the decline to sea-level rise and coastal erosion between about 850 and 1000. The event is now rated `scholarly_interpretation` and both views are noted on it. It lies outside the campaign window.
- Casualty figures given by Abu Zayd for the 879 Guangzhou massacre are not independently verifiable and are not quoted as fact.

### Melaka, 1460 CE
- **Founding date** (c. 1400) is conventional; the first firm records are Ming entries of 1403–1405.
- **Regnal dates** of Mansur Shah (c. 1459–1477) and Tun Perak's ministry (1456–1498) derive from the Sejarah Melayu and later scholarship; checked October 2026 against *Britannica* and standard ruler lists, which give the same spans (Muzaffar Shah's death is placed "about 1459"). Kept as `scholarly_interpretation` because the chronicle is the ultimate source.
- **Parameswara / Iskandar Shah** identity question is noted in the figure entry.
- **Undang-Undang Laut Melaka** is traditionally placed in Mahmud Shah's reign; the game offers it as an acquirable capability rather than a dated event.
- **Ternate and Brunei** polities before 1500 are thinly documented; both are rated `uncertain`.
- **Ayutthaya and Pegu** lay inland; their map positions are the cities, reached by river.
- **Currency**: Melaka used tin coinage alongside gold, silver and Chinese cash; the game's "trade cash" is an abstract unit, not a historical price level.
- **Jong dimensions and capacities** follow Manguin's conservative estimates; Portuguese reports of much larger vessels are noted but not used for balance.

- **Abbasid reconquest of Oman, 893**: checked October 2026. The campaign of 280 AH / 893 CE under Muḥammad ibn Nūr, following the civil war that began with the deposition of Imam al-Ṣalt in 885, ended the Ibadi imamate and reincorporated much of Oman as an Abbasid province; Suhar's residents are reported to have fled in large numbers. Rated `established`; the game's fee increase at Suhar is the simulated consequence.

### Kilwa, 1330 CE
- **Regnal dates** of al-Hasan ibn Sulayman and his successors rest on the Kilwa Chronicle (written c. 1520–1530) and the coin series; the sultan's death is placed c. 1333 and rated `uncertain`.
- **Husuni Kubwa and the mosque extension** are dated to his reign by coins and architectural comparison (`scholarly_interpretation`).
- **Sofala**: the medieval town is not securely located; the marker is the later Portuguese Sofala. The chronicle's story of how Kilwa acquired Sofala is a later retrospective.
- **Zanzibar island**: Tumbatu and Kizimkazi are the documented medieval towns; which harbour served the trade in the 1330s is unknown, so the port is rated `uncertain` and its location `disputed`.
- **Black Death**: documented at Alexandria from autumn 1347 and in Yemen after 1351; its spread to the Swahili coast is unknown, so the event affects only Aden.
- **Enslaved people** were traded on this coast. The game does not represent them as cargo; the scenario's uncertainty note says so.
- **Ship classes** are reconstructions: no fourteenth-century Swahili hull has been excavated, and the mtepe is documented in detail only from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

### Venice, 1423 CE
- **Mocenigo's oration** survives only in later chronicles; its figures are not independently verifiable and are not used for balance.
- **Thessalonica 1423**: whether Venice bought the city or accepted it as a protectorate is debated; the Venetian "sale" story is treated as unfounded by recent scholarship.
- **Great galleys** were state-built and auctioned; a private house could not own one. The game turns winning the auction into a technology and a ship class, which is a simplification.
- **Round ship dimensions** follow Lane's ranges; speeds and capacities are gameplay values.
- **Mediterranean winds** use a single 12-month table (rated `reconstruction`); the bora, sirocco and mistral are not modelled.
- **Constantinople** closes on 29 May 1453 and reopens as a restricted Ottoman port on the treaty of 18 April 1454; the two per cent customs duty of the treaty is reflected only as a fee multiplier.
- **Thessalonica after 1430** reopens restricted in 1431 as a simplification of the peace of 1430 and the Venetian withdrawal completed by 1432.

### Carreira da Índia, 1505 CE
- **Bibliography**: Boxer (1969), Pearson (1987), Newitt (2005), the Hakluyt *Commentaries* (1875–1884) and Subrahmanyam's 2012 second edition were confirmed against catalogue and publisher records in October 2026; the 1993 Longman first edition of Subrahmanyam, the University of Minnesota Press imprint of Diffie and Winius, and the 1921 second volume of Dames's *Barbosa* are recorded from the implementer's knowledge and say so on the entry.
- **Dates checked**: Almeida's departure (25 March 1505), the capture of Kilwa (22 July 1505, fort built in seventeen days), Sofala's fort (September to November 1505), the 1507 Mozambique fort, Diu (3 February 1509), Goa (25 November 1510), Hormuz (1515), the withdrawal from Kilwa (1512), Manuel I's death (13 December 1521), Gama's death at Cochin (24 December 1524), the Antwerp factory (moved from Bruges in 1499), the Diu fort (1535) and siege (4 September to 6 November 1538). The 1513 Calicut treaty date, the Zaragoza signing (22 April 1529) and Ridaniya (22 January 1517) follow the standard accounts and were not re-checked.
- **The 1505 investors** were the Welser, Fugger, Höchstetter and Gossembrot of Augsburg and the Imhoff and Hirschvogel of Nuremberg with Genoese and Florentine partners; Marchionni financed the 1501 fleet. The "Florentine house of Lisbon" rival is inspired by him, not a record of his firm.
- **Ships**: the nau's 400 tons follows the Flor de la Mar; the caravel, galleon and Malabar paráu are reconstructions, and the early galleon's form is debated.
- **Cannanore** is grouped under the Cochin polity record for relations; the Kolathiri kingdom was distinct, and the port record says so.
- **Wind**: the Indian Ocean monsoon table is used for the whole map; the Atlantic trade winds are not modelled and the scenario note says so.
- **Enslaved people** were traded at Mina and in the Estado; the game does not represent them as cargo and the scenario note says so.

### The Company's Monopoly (Dutch), 1602 CE
- **Bibliography**: Gaastra, *The Dutch East India Company: Expansion and Decline* (Walburg Pers, 2003), Israel, *Dutch Primacy in World Trade 1585–1740* (Clarendon, 1989), Bruijn, Gaastra and Schöffer, *Dutch-Asiatic Shipping in the 17th and 18th Centuries* (RGP, 1979–1987) and Glamann, *Dutch-Asiatic Trade 1620–1740* (1958; reprinted 1981) were confirmed against catalogue and publisher records in October 2026; Boxer, *The Dutch Seaborne Empire 1600–1800* (1965), Andaya, *The World of Maluku* (1993), Parthesius, *Dutch Ships in Tropical Waters* (2010) and Blussé, *Strange Company* (1986) are recorded from the implementer's knowledge and say so on the entry.
- **Dates checked**: the capture of Amboina (22 February 1605), the taking of the *Santa Catarina* (25 February 1603), the Hirado trading pass (20 September 1609), the storming of Jayakarta (30 May 1619), the surrender of Malacca (14 January 1641), the landing at the Cape (6 April 1652), the Masulipatnam factory (1605), the fort at Pulicat (1610), the Mocha factory (1621) and Mataram's sieges of Batavia (1628 and 1629). The charter date (20 March 1602), the Truce (9 April 1609), the Amboyna executions (9 March 1623), the Surat farman (1616), the Gamron opening (1623), the move to Dejima (1641) and the Peace of Münster (30 January 1648) follow the standard accounts and were not re-checked.
- **The Company's monopoly** made private Dutch trade east of the Cape illegal; the player's house is modelled as a freighter and subscriber working under the charter, which is a gameplay fiction, and the scenario note says so. The English, Chinese and Gujarati rivals are reconstructions of documented communities, not of particular firms.
- **Ships**: the fluyt's 300 tons and the retourschip's 600 tons are round figures within the documented ranges; the jacht's 120 tons is a reconstruction. All three use the game's cog and carrack hull builders, not period-accurate Dutch hull forms.
- **Zeelandia** is given no polity record: the Company held the south-western plain of Formosa from 1624 to 1662 and no Chinese state ruled the island, so the port is marked closed until the fort is built and its extent is dated to those years.
- **Vijayanagara** survived at Vellore and Chandragiri until 1646; Pulicat's grant came from the local nayaka under that fading suzerainty, and the extent is drawn as suzerainty only, with the relation record kept on the Vijayanagara polity for simplicity.
- **Wind**: the Indian Ocean monsoon table is used for the whole map; the westerlies of Brouwer's route and the Atlantic trade winds are not modelled and the scenario note says so.
- **Banda** was a massacre, and enslaved people worked the Company's plantations and Batavia; the game does not represent them as cargo and the scenario and event records say so.

### Fleets of the Western Ocean (Ming), 1405 CE
- **Bibliography**: Levathes, *When China Ruled the Seas* (Simon & Schuster, 1994, 252 pp.), Church, "Zheng He: An Investigation into the Plausibility of 450-ft Treasure Ships" (*Monumenta Serica* 53, 2005, pp. 1–43), Wade, "The Zheng He Voyages: A Reassessment" (ARI Working Paper 31, 2004) and Fei Xin, *Hsing-ch'a Sheng-lan* (Mills and Ptak, Harrassowitz 1996, 153 pp.) were confirmed against catalogue, journal and publisher records in October 2026; Dreyer (2007) and Ma Huan in Mills (1970) were confirmed for the Melaka campaign. Sen's 2006 *JESHO* article, Needham's *Science and Civilisation* vol. 4 pt 3 (1971) and the *Cambridge History of China* vol. 7 (1988) are recorded from the implementer's knowledge and say so on the entry; the JMBRAS printing of Wade is likewise from memory.
- **Dates checked**: the order for the first voyage (11 July 1405, with the alternative tradition of a June departure from Nanjing noted), the seventh voyage (1431–1433) and the fleet's return in summer 1433, the start of the tally trade (1404), its lapse after Yoshimitsu's death in 1408 with the last mission of 1410 and the rebuffed envoys of 1417 and 1419, and its resumption under Yoshinori in 1432. An earlier draft dated the lapse to 1411; the event record now states the 1408–1410 sequence and keeps 1411 only as the date the game applies the closure. The Galle stele date (15 February 1409), Chen Zuyi (1407), Alakeshvara (1411), Parameswara at Nanjing (1411), Hormuz (1414), the giraffe (1414), Sekandar (1415), Beijing (1421), Yongle's death (12 August 1424), Hongxi's edict (7 September 1424), Shō Hashi (1429) and Xuande's death (31 January 1435) follow Dreyer and the standard accounts and were not re-checked.
- **Zheng He's death** is given as 1433 or 1435 in different traditions; the event record says so and the campaign does not fix it.
- **Treasure ship size** follows Church's argument for ships of about 60–75 m rather than the Ming History's 44 by 18 zhang; the ship entry and the README say so. The sand ship, Guangdong junk, Ryukyu junk and Malabar sambūk are reconstructions.
- **The sea ban**: private overseas trade was illegal under the haijin, so the player's house is a fiction working under tribute tallies, and the scenario note says so. Rivals are reconstructions of documented communities (Ryukyu court traders, Fujian private shippers, Calicut's Muslim merchants), not particular firms.
- **Malindi** is given no polity record; it was an independent town whose rulers are not named in the Chinese sources.
- **Kotte**: Ceylon's political geography in 1405 (Alakeshvara at Raigama and Kotte, Gampola in the highlands) is simplified to one polity, and the extent is marked approximate.
- **Wind**: the Straits monsoon table is used for the whole map, including the Arabian Sea and the East China Sea, and the scenario note says so.

### Harbour of the Maharaja (Srivijaya), 992 CE
- **Bibliography**: Coedès, *The Indianized States of Southeast Asia* (East-West Center Press, 1968, xxi + 403 pp.), Wolters, *Early Indonesian Commerce* (Cornell, 1967, 404 pp.), Nilakanta Sastri, *The Cōḷas* (University of Madras, 2nd ed. 1955), Kulke, Kesavapany and Sakhuja (eds.), *Nagapattinam to Suvarnadwipa* (ISEAS, 2009, ISBN 978-981-230-937-2), Heng, *Sino-Malay Trade and Diplomacy* (Ohio University Press, 2009), Hall, *Maritime Trade and State Development in Early Southeast Asia* (University of Hawaii Press, 1985, xvi + 368 pp.), Bronson's "Exchange at the Upstream and Downstream Ends" (Hutterer ed., Michigan Papers 13, pp. 39–52; dated 1977 or 1978 in different records), Jacq-Hergoualc'h, *The Malay Peninsula* (Brill, 2002, xxxv + 607 pp.), Flecker, *The Archaeological Excavation of the 10th Century Intan Shipwreck* (BAR S1047, 2002), Christie, "The Medieval Tamil-language Inscriptions in Southeast Asia and China" (*JSEAS* 29(2), 1998, pp. 239–268) and Spencer, *The Politics of Expansion* (New Era, Madras, 1983, viii + 199 pp.) were confirmed against catalogue, publisher and journal records in November 2026. Hirth and Rockhill's *Chau Ju-kua* (1911), Chattopadhyaya's *Atīśa and Tibet* (1967) and the *Epigraphia Indica* 22 edition of the Larger Leiden plates are recorded from the implementer's knowledge and say so on the entry; the plates' content was confirmed against the Leiden digital collection and published summaries.
- **Dates checked**: the Srivijayan envoy's report of the Javanese attack at the Song court in spring 992; the embassy of 1003 announcing the temple for the emperor's long life; Rajaraja's grant of Anaimangalam in his twenty-first year (c. 1006) to the vihara built by Cudamani Varmadeva and completed by Maravijayottungavarman; Tambralinga's embassies of 1001, 1016 and 1070; the Chola embassies of 1015 and 1033 (and the failed one of 1020); Atiśa's twelve years in Suvarnadvipa; Airlangga's consecration in 1019; the Chola expedition of about 1025 and the list of places in Rajendra's inscriptions; the Guangzhou trade office of 971 and the Quanzhou office of 1087. The embassies of 1008 (Maravijayottungavarman), 1017 (Haji Sumatrabhumi) and 1028 (Sri Deva) follow Coedès's reading of the *Song shi* and were not re-checked against the Chinese text; Puni's embassies of 977 and 1082 and the Kamalagyan inscription of 1037 follow the standard accounts.
- **The Javanese disaster**: the Pucangan (Calcutta Stone) inscription of 1041 describes the destruction of Dharmawangsa's court by the lord of Wurawari during a royal wedding. Coedès dated it to 1006; the usual reading now is 1016, and the game triggers it in 1016 with both readings on the record. Srivijaya's hand behind Wurawari is a historians' inference and is labelled so.
- **Srivijaya's capital**: the identification of the eleventh-century capital with Palembang (rather than Jambi, where the court was by the late eleventh century) is the usual view and is rated `scholarly_interpretation`; the port's location is `approximate`.
- **Kadaram, Takuapa, Lamuri, Barus and the Javanese port** are placed at the Bujang valley, Ko Kho Khao, Lamreh, Lobu Tua and the Brantas delta respectively; all are `approximate` or `disputed`, and the rulers of Takuapa, Lamuri and Barus in this period are unknown (their port records carry no polity). Tambralinga's identification with Zhao Rukuo's Danmaling is disputed and noted. Puni is known only from the Chinese accounts and is rated `uncertain`.
- **Sangrama Vijayottungavarman** is named in the Chola inscriptions as the captured king; whether he is the "Haji Sumatrabhumi" of the 1017 embassy, and what became of him, are unknown.
- **Ships**: the lashed-lug tradition is documented by the Punjulharjo, Intan and Cirebon hulls; the sizes of the coaster, the kunlun trader and the Javanese jong are reconstructions, and the "great kunlun ship" rests on Chinese literary descriptions of fifty-metre vessels for which no hull has been found (`uncertain`). The Coromandel merchantman and the Gulf ship of the Guangzhou quarter are reconstructions. All use the game's lancaran, jong and sewn-plank hull builders.
- **Goods**: Barus camphor, Sumatran gold, benzoin, gharuwood, lakawood, tin and the Chinese exports follow Zhao Rukuo (1225) and the tenth-century wreck cargoes; Zhao wrote two centuries after the campaign, and the records say so. Chola cottons are rated `scholarly_interpretation`.
- **Currency**: the gold māsa is a weight unit of the Javanese and Malay inscriptions; treasury values in it are a gameplay abstraction.
- **Wind**: the Straits monsoon table is used for the whole map, including the Bay of Bengal and the China coast, and the scenario note says so.
- **Enslaved people** were traded in these waters; the game does not represent them as cargo and the scenario note says so.

## Map layers
Territorial outlines in `territories.ts` are coarse approximations for orientation, each with a control type, period, evidence rating and sources; none is a surveyed boundary and the map labels every one approximate. The dated changes within campaigns (the Zanj in lower Iraq 869–883; Thessalonica Byzantine to 1423, Venetian 1423–1430, Ottoman from 1430; Cyprus under Mamluk tribute from 1426; Constantinople Ottoman from 1453; the Morea from 1460; Brescia and Bergamo Venetian from 1428; Gujarat independent from 1407) follow the standard accounts cited on each extent. The Roman campaign adds the empire's western, African and eastern provinces and Arabia Petraea as coarse outlines, with Aksum, Himyar, the Chera kingdom and the Western Kshatrapas. The Song campaign adds the Srivijayan court at Jambi, Goryeo, Heian Japan, Kediri, Lý Đại Việt, Ma-i, Butuan and the Jin, with the Song, Srivijaya, Chola, Champa and Puni outlines shared into the window. The East India Company campaign adds Great Britain, St Helena, Company Bengal from 1765, Madras and the Northern Circars, Bombay, Bencoolen, Penang from 1786, Kedah, Qing China, Macao, Mysore before and after 1792, the Maratha sphere, the nominal Mughal suzerainty, French Pondicherry in its two restored periods, Danish Tranquebar, the Spanish Philippines and British-occupied Manila. The Srivijaya campaign adds the river-mouth core at Palembang and Jambi, a reduced sphere after 1025, the Chola country, Chola Sri Lanka from 993, the Chola claim over Kadaram after 1025, the Song, Medang to 1016 and Kahuripan from 1019, Tambralinga and Puni. Extents rated `uncertain` (Ziyadid Yemen, Abbasid Oman after 893, the fourteenth-century Swahili towns, Pasai, Calicut, Ternate, Banda, Brunei) mark polities whose territorial reach is thinly documented. Inland settlements are shown for context with their own periods; Great Zimbabwe's occupation dates follow the conventional c. 1100–1450.

## Territory model
Regions are groupings of each scenario's ports made for the influence summary and are rated `reconstruction`. Each campaign's territorial rule records its basis: Gulf merchant communities abroad in the ninth century (Tharisappalli plates, the Accounts of China and India) for Siraf; Swahili towns founded by merchant lineages and Kilwa's hold on Sofala (the Kilwa Chronicle, Sutton, Pearson) for Kilwa; Venetian fondaci and consulates and the Senate's monopoly of overseas possessions (Lane, Ashtor) for Venice; the foreign merchant communities of the Straits (Pires) for Melaka, where a house holding a port in the sultan's name is a reconstruction; the Srivijayan vihara at Nagapattinam (Leiden plates) and the Tamil guild settlements (Christie) for Srivijaya, where Wolters's reading of the maharaja's ring of ports held through local chiefs underlies the dependency rule. Settlements and dependencies, influence scores and their thresholds are gameplay abstractions.

## Wind model
The Siraf, Srivijaya, Song, Kilwa, Ming, Melaka, Carreira, Dutch Company and East India Company scenarios use simplified 12-month wind tables (direction and speed) representing the north-east and south-west monsoons with transitional months. They reproduce the seasonal rhythm described by Hourani, Chaudhuri and Reid but are not meteorological reconstructions. The Venice and Roman scenarios use a single Mediterranean table (westerlies, winter gales, the summer etesians) rated `reconstruction`; for the Roman campaign it also covers the Red Sea and the Arabian Sea, where the monsoon is represented only by the Hippalus technology.

## Sea lanes
The routing graph (`src/data/sealanes.ts`) is a gameplay abstraction that keeps routes at sea and respects the absence of the Suez (1869) and Panama (1914) canals in earlier periods; Trajan's canal from the Nile to the Red Sea is usable only from 112 to 767. It is not a nautical chart.
